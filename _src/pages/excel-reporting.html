The usual starting point

Rebuilding a workbook nobody quite trusts any more.

Most Excel work we do isn't starting from a blank sheet — it's an inherited workbook that's grown one exception at a time: a formula someone patched by hand for one bad month and never fixed, a hidden column holding the whole thing together, a total that's been slightly wrong for so long everyone's stopped checking it. We take that apart, understand what it's actually meant to calculate, and rebuild it so it does that reliably.

Worth checking

Signs your workbook needs rebuilding, not just patching.

  • Two people run the same report and get two different totals.
  • You've caught a wrong figure before and are no longer sure it hasn't happened again.
  • Only one person in the business can safely make changes to it.
  • Adding a new row, product or month means editing formulas rather than just typing data.
  • It prints badly, or looks fine on screen but confusing on paper or in a PDF.
  • Conditional formatting relies on colour alone, so a colour-blind reader can't tell good from bad.
Formatting that actually communicates

Conditional formatting and variance that reads correctly for everyone.

Colour is useful, but colour alone is a trap — it disappears in black-and-white print, and it's meaningless to a colour-blind reader. We build variance and status formatting so the meaning is always carried by more than colour.

Signed numbers and arrows

A variance shows as +6.9% or −4.1%, with a direction arrow, not just a green or red cell. The number and the symbol carry the meaning, so the colour is a bonus, not the whole message.

Print and PDF checked

Reports are checked in greyscale and on paper before they're handed over, not just on your screen — so the version that reaches a board pack or a client still makes sense.

Excel, or move to Power BI?

Excel is often the right answer — here's when to move on from it.

We won't push you towards Power BI because it's more impressive. Excel is the right tool for a lot of real work, and moving something to Power BI before it needs to be there just adds complexity.

Stay in Excel when…

  • It's a model people need to type figures into, not just view
  • One or two people use it, in the same layout each time
  • The data is small enough to sit comfortably in a workbook

Move to Power BI when…

  • Several people need their own filtered view of the same report
  • Someone is manually copying data into the workbook from a system every week
  • The file has got slow, heavy, or genuinely too large to work in comfortably
