A fixed price to build, a retainer to keep it running

Report work splits into two kinds of cost, and it's worth keeping them separate in your head before you ask anyone for a number.

The build is a one-off: connecting to your sources, working out the logic, laying the report out, and checking the numbers tie. That's quoted as a fixed price, agreed before anything starts, so you know exactly what it costs before you commit.

The upkeep is different. A report that refreshes on a schedule still needs someone watching it — a source changes its export format, a formula needs extending when the business adds a new branch, a refresh fails overnight and needs fixing before Monday morning. That's ongoing work, so it's priced as a monthly retainer rather than a one-off fee.

Both start the same way: a first conversation and a look at your data, at no cost and with no obligation. That's how I work out which kind of job it is and roughly what it will take, before either of me commits to anything.

What drives the price

Five things move a quote more than anything else. None of them are about how big your business is — a small business with messy data can cost more to sort out than a larger one with clean data.

  • Number of sources. One spreadsheet is a different job to five spreadsheets, two logins and an email export. Every extra source is another connection to build and another thing that can change format later.
  • How messy the data is. Consistent column headers, sensible date formats and no merged cells make a build quick. Free-text fields, inconsistent naming and manual workarounds someone built years ago make it slower, because those quirks have to be understood before they can be automated.
  • Whether the logic is documented. If you can explain how a number is currently calculated, that's a head start. If the calculation exists only in one person's memory, or across several tabs nobody fully trusts, working it out and confirming it's right takes longer than building the report itself.
  • How often it runs. A report built once for a single meeting is cheaper than one that has to keep working, unattended, every week or every day — because the second one needs error-handling and monitoring the first one doesn't.
  • How many people need it. A report for one person's own use is simpler than one that has to serve several audiences with different permissions, different views, or different levels of detail.

A quote reflects where your job sits on each of these, not a generic day rate applied to every project regardless of what it involves.

The three retainer tiers

For ongoing upkeep and support, there are three fixed monthly tiers. Each includes a set number of hours a month; the monthly fee divided by the hours gives the effective hourly rate, which falls as the tier goes up.

Tier Monthly fee Hours included Effective rate Typically suits
Essential £400 Up to 5 £80.00/hr One or two reports, light monitoring
Standard £720 Up to 10 £72.00/hr Several reports, occasional small changes
Priority £1,100 Up to 16 £68.75/hr Multiple reports, frequent changes, faster turnaround
Effective rate is the monthly fee divided by the included hours — for example £720 ÷ 10 hours = £72.00/hr. It falls as the included hours rise.

There's no minimum term. A retainer is rolling monthly, cancelled with 30 days' notice, so committing to one isn't a long-term contract — it's a decision you can revisit every month if your circumstances change.

Work beyond the included hours

Some months need more than the tier's included hours — a new source gets added, a report gets extended, or something breaks that takes longer than usual to fix. That extra time is billed at £80 an hour, or quoted as a fixed price up front if it's a distinct enough piece of work to plan for rather than just extra hours.

Hours that go unused in a given month don't roll over into the next one, unless that's been agreed in writing beforehand — the retainer buys availability and capacity each month, not a bank of hours to draw down whenever.

Doing nothing, automating, or hiring

There are really three options on the table, and it's worth being honest about what each one actually costs.

Doing nothing costs whatever time someone already spends rebuilding the report by hand, plus the risk that comes with a manual process — a missed week, a copy-paste error, a person who leaves and takes the method with them. If that person spends, say, three hours a week on it, that's 3 × 46 = 138 hours a year, allowing for around six weeks of holiday and quieter periods. That time has a cost even though no invoice ever arrives for it.

Automating it trades that ongoing manual time for a fixed build cost plus a retainer. Take the Standard tier as an example: 10 hours a month is 10 × 12 = 120 hours a year of support and changes — a broadly similar order of magnitude to the 138 hours above, except most of it goes on genuine changes and questions rather than rebuilding the same report from scratch every week, because the rebuilding itself is what gets automated away.

Hiring someone to do this in-house is a fixed ongoing cost — salary, employer National Insurance, pension contribution, holiday cover, equipment, management time — that exists whether there's five hours of report work that month or fifteen. A retainer scales with the hours actually needed and can be cancelled with 30 days' notice; a hire generally can't be turned up and down the same way.

None of this is a promise about what automating your particular report will save — that depends entirely on how it's currently built and how often it runs. If you want to work through your own numbers rather than an illustrative example, the hours-saved calculator lets you put in your own figures.

How invoicing and notice work

Retainers are invoiced at the start of the month, payable within 14 days. There's no long-term contract behind it — it runs on a rolling monthly basis, and either side can end it with 30 days' notice. One-off builds are quoted and agreed as a fixed price before work starts, so there's no surprise invoice at the end.
