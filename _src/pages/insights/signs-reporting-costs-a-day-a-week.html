The arithmetic behind "a day a week"

Before the five signs, the working. Assume a standard working day of 7.5 hours. The five signs below carry a rough, illustrative time cost of: 3 hours rebuilding by hand, 1.5 hours reconciling two systems, 1 hour waiting on and chasing numbers, and 2 hours re-checking a total nobody trusts. Add those up:

3 + 1.5 + 1 + 2 = 7.5 hours — one full working day, every week.

Over a working year of 46 weeks (allowing roughly six weeks for holidays and quieter periods), that's 7.5 × 46 = 345 hours, or 345 ÷ 7.5 = 46 working days a year. These are illustrative figures to show the method, not a claim about your business specifically — the hours-saved calculator lets you put your own numbers through the same sum.

1. It gets rebuilt by hand, every time

Why it happens. The sources were never connected to the report directly, so someone exports, copies, pastes and reformats the same data into the same layout on the same schedule — because that's how it was first built, and nobody has had the time to change it since.

What it costs. In this example, 3 hours a week. It's rarely counted as a cost because it doesn't appear on an invoice, but the hours are real, and they're the same 3 hours every week regardless of whether anything in the underlying data actually changed.

What fixing it looks like. The sources get connected once, and the report refreshes itself on a schedule. The rebuilding step disappears rather than getting faster — there's nothing left to do by hand.

2. Two systems disagree

Why it happens. Two systems that should show the same figure — sales in the CRM versus sales in the finance system, say — drift apart because of timing differences, different definitions, or a manual step that only updates one of them.

What it costs. In this example, 1.5 hours a week spent checking whether the gap is real or just timing, and explaining it to whoever asks. That's before counting the cost of a decision made on the wrong number while the gap went unnoticed.

What fixing it looks like. A standing reconciliation that runs automatically and flags the records that don't match, with the reason why — so the question gets answered once, by the report, instead of being investigated fresh each time someone notices a mismatch.

3. One person owns it

Why it happens. Someone built the report at some point, understood it best, and has quietly kept maintaining it ever since. It works — right up until that person is on leave, busy with something else, or leaves the business.

What it costs. This one is harder to put a weekly figure on, because most weeks it costs nothing extra — until the week it doesn't. The cost shows up as a spike: a report that's late, wrong, or simply doesn't happen, at the exact moment the one person who understands it isn't available.

What fixing it looks like. The logic gets documented in plain language, not just encoded in formulas one person can read. Anyone competent should be able to pick the report up, not just the person who built it.

4. Decisions wait for the numbers

Why it happens. The report only gets built after someone asks for it, so there's a gap — sometimes hours, sometimes days — between the question being asked and the numbers being ready to answer it.

What it costs. In this example, 1 hour a week of chasing, waiting, and rescheduling meetings around when the numbers will actually be ready. The larger, harder-to-quantify cost is the decision itself slipping later than it needed to.

What fixing it looks like. The report already exists and is already current by the time someone needs it, because it refreshes on its own schedule rather than on demand.

5. Nobody fully trusts the total

Why it happens. The report has been wrong before — a formula error, a duplicate row, a total that didn't reconcile — and that memory sticks, even after the immediate problem gets fixed.

What it costs. In this example, 2 hours a week re-checking figures by hand before they're presented, just in case. That's a genuine cost, and it's a reasonable response to a report that's let people down before — the issue is the report, not the caution.

What fixing it looks like. A built-in reconciliation check on every refresh, so the report proves its own total ties rather than asking anyone to take it on trust. Confidence gets rebuilt by the report checking itself, consistently, not by one more manual check this week.
