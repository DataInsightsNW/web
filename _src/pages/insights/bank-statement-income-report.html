What is a bank statement income report?

A plain explanation of the deliverable: turning raw bank statements into a categorised, readable picture of income and outgoings — and what it deliberately is not.

What the report is

A bank statement income report takes raw statement data — pages of individual transactions, in whatever format the bank exports them — and turns it into a structured, categorised summary: what came in, what went out, and what that looked like month by month. The statements themselves are a long list of dated line items in whatever order the bank produced them. The report is the same information, organised so a person can actually read it and answer a specific question with it.

What it's used for

The most common uses are:

  • Affordability checks. Building a realistic picture of regular income against regular outgoings, for a specific decision that depends on it.
  • Lending support. Giving a lender or broker a clear, categorised record of account activity to support an application, in a format that's quicker to review than raw statements.
  • Self-employed income verification. Self-employed income often doesn't arrive as a single, regular salary line — it can be several payments a month, from different sources, of different sizes. A categorised report separates genuine trading income from transfers, refunds and one-off items, so the underlying pattern is visible.
  • A true picture of the money. Sometimes the point isn't a specific application at all — it's simply wanting an honest, organised view of what's actually coming in and going out, month by month, without doing the categorising by hand.

What it contains

Section What it shows
Monthly summary Total income and total outgoings for each month covered, side by side
Income breakdown Income grouped by source or type, rather than as one undifferentiated total
Outgoings breakdown Spending grouped into categories, so regular commitments stand apart from one-off or discretionary spending
Recurring items Payments and receipts that repeat on a regular cycle, identified as such
Notes on irregularities Anything unusual or hard to categorise, flagged plainly rather than silently folded into a category it doesn't really belong in

What it is not

This needs to be stated plainly rather than buried in small print: a bank statement income report is a data product, not regulated financial advice, and not a lending or affordability decision in itself. I organise and present the transaction data — I don't assess whether someone can afford a loan, and I don't make a lending or credit decision. Whoever needs a formal affordability assessment or a regulated financial recommendation should get that from a person or firm authorised to give it. The report is an input to that kind of decision, not the decision itself.

How the data is handled

Bank statements are about as sensitive as personal data gets, and they're handled accordingly, in line with UK GDPR. In practice that means:

  • A clear basis for holding the data — statements are only requested and processed for the specific report agreed with you, not for any other purpose.
  • Only what's needed. I work with the statement data required to produce the report, not more than that.
  • Limited access. Only the person doing the work has access to the statements while the report is being produced.
  • No onward sharing. Statement data isn't passed to any third party beyond what's specifically agreed as part of the work — for example, sending the finished report to a lender you've named.
  • An agreed retention period. How long the underlying statement data is kept is agreed with you up front, and it's deleted once that period ends rather than kept indefinitely "just in case".
  • Your rights stay yours. Under UK GDPR you can ask what personal data is held about you and ask for it to be corrected or deleted, subject to the normal exceptions — for example, where I'm required to keep records for a period for legal or accounting reasons.

What I need to produce one

Two things: the statement data itself — usually as a PDF, CSV or bank export, covering whichever period the report needs to reflect — from the account holder or with their clear authorisation to use it, and a plain answer to what the report needs to show and who it's for. That second part matters more than it sounds: a report built for a mortgage broker and one built for someone's own peace of mind about their spending don't need to look the same, even from identical statements.

Turnaround

There's no single turnaround time, because the job isn't a fixed size. A single account covering three months of statements is a different amount of work to five years of history across four accounts with mixed personal and business transactions on the same statements. Categorising is also slower where the statements are scanned images rather than a clean export, or where transaction descriptions are generic and need cross-checking against other information to categorise properly.

Rather than quote a generic figure that wouldn't be accurate either way, I agree a realistic timeframe as part of the same initial conversation that scopes the report — based on how many accounts, how many months, and the format the statements arrive in.

What this means for you

If you need a clear, categorised view of income and outgoings from bank statements — whether that's to support an application, verify self-employed income, or simply to understand where the money actually goes — that's exactly what this report is for. It won't tell you whether a lending decision should be made, and it isn't a substitute for regulated financial advice, but it will give whoever does need to make that decision a far clearer set of numbers to work from.
